# Northmere Coastal Engineering: East Quay seawall trial, monitoring report for the first winter

Synthetic test material for SOAR's E0 extractor fixture. Every name, place, date and figure in this document is invented.

Report NCE-HHB-114, revision B, dated 2 April 2034. Prepared for the Halverstrand Harbour Board.

## 1. Summary

The contractor handed the new East Quay seawall over to the Board on 12 September 2033. This report covers its first winter, from 1 October 2033 to 15 March 2034.

Across the eight storms in which the wall was overtopped, mean overtopping fell from 4.6 litres per second per metre in the pre-trial winter of 2032–33 to 1.3 litres per second per metre. The peak single reading fell from 31 to 9 litres per second per metre.

The quay road was closed on 2 days, both during storm S3. In the pre-trial winter it was closed on 11 days.

We recommend a second winter of monitoring before the Board decides whether to extend the wall.

## 2. The wall and the gauges

The trial section is 140 metres long. Its stepped face rises in six steps of 0.45 metres to a recurved crest at 4.1 metres above chart datum. The crest returns 0.6 metres seaward, and its lip is cast with a drip groove so that water thrown up the face falls back on the steps.

Three overtopping gauges were fixed to the crest at chainages 20, 70 and 120 metres, named G1, G2 and G3. Each gauge is a tipping tray behind a slot in the crest. It logs the volume it catches every ten seconds, and the logger reports overtopping as litres per second per metre of wall.

Monitoring began on 1 September 2033, two days after the contractor completed the wall. The logger also records the tide level, the significant wave height from the harbour wave buoy, and the wind at the harbour office.

The pre-trial baseline comes from two older gauges of a different model. They were mounted on the timber wall at chainages 35 and 105 metres during the 2032–33 winter and removed when the works began.

## 3. Storm events

We count a storm as any tide in which at least one gauge recorded overtopping above 1.0 litre per second per metre. Eight storms met that test.

Storm S3 was the largest of the winter. It arrived on a spring tide with a north-easterly gale, and the tide level at the harbour office reached 2.71 metres above chart datum. Green water crossed the crest at G1 for about four minutes around high water. The road was closed as a precaution for the tides of 26 and 27 November.

In every storm the western end of the wall (G1) took the most water. The western end faces the harbour mouth, and the waves there arrive less refracted than at the eastern end, which sits in the lee of the breakwater.

The pre-trial winter had eleven storms by the same test. Its largest, on 4 January 2033, gave a peak of 31 litres per second per metre at the western baseline gauge.

## 4. Effects on the quay

The quay road carries the harbour's only access to the fish market and the lifeboat station. Before the trial, the Board closed it whenever a high tide coincided with a forecast gale. During the trial winter the Board kept the same rule but reopened the road once the gauges showed no overtopping for two consecutive readings.

Harbour staff reported far less shingle thrown onto the road. They cleared the road of stones twice this winter, against nine times in the pre-trial winter. We did not measure the shingle, so these counts come from the staff's work log.

## 5. Cost

The Board's final account for the trial was 2.9 million crowns. This includes 0.3 million crowns for design changes to the crest after the first model tests, when the original crest returned too little water to the steps.

The regional coastal fund's share was fixed at 1.82 million crowns before the design changes. The Board paid the balance from its harbour dues and a short-term loan, which it expects to repay from dues by 2036.

The cost of monitoring, 0.11 million crowns for the first winter, is not part of the final account. It is paid from the Board's maintenance budget.

## 6. Limitations

- Gauge G2 failed between 14 and 30 November 2033 because of a cable fault. Storms S2 and S3 therefore have readings from G1 and G3 only, and their means are taken over two gauges.
- One winter of data cannot separate the effect of the wall from that of a calmer or rougher season. The 2033–34 winter had fewer north-easterly gales than the ten-year average: seven, against an average of eleven.
- The baseline gauges were a different model, sited at different chainages and fixed lower on the old wall. Their readings and ours are not directly comparable, and we have not corrected for the difference.
- The gauges measure water crossing the crest. They do not measure water reaching the road, which is what the road closures depend on.
- The logger's clock ran six minutes fast until 1 December 2033. We have not corrected the times in the logger's raw export.

## 7. Other observations

Sand built up against the two lowest steps over the winter, to a depth of about 0.3 metres at the eastern end. It has not yet reached the third step. We recommend surveying it each spring.

A pair of rock pipits nested in a drainage gap behind the crest at chainage 95 metres. The Board's ecologist has asked that the gap be left open until the end of the nesting season.

Two residents of the quay cottages reported a loud booming from the crest during storm S3. We made no noise measurements, so we cannot say whether the recurved crest is louder than the timber wall it replaced.

The drip groove on the crest lip filled with weed by February. Harbour staff cleared it in early March, and we recommend clearing it each autumn before the first gales.

## 8. Recommendations

1. Keep all three gauges in place for a second winter, with a spare cable for G2.
2. Survey the sand on the lowest steps each spring.
3. Before any extension, compare the second winter's storms with the first, storm by storm, at matched tide levels and wave heights.
4. Record road closures with the time the road reopened, so that the closures can be compared with the gauge readings.
